Introduction

The black capuchin monkey (Sapajus nigritus) inhabits the Atlantic Forest, including forest fragments and production landscapes like pine plantations. Their ability to thrive in disturbed environments makes them a key species for understanding primate adaptation to human-altered habitats. Black capuchins often bark-strip pine trees to consume inner tissues, however ecology of primates in poorly understood in production landscapes. This gap on knowladge difficult overall managment practices that promote coexistence between production and primates populations.

Bark-Stripping Behavior

Primates are known for their curious and adaptive nature. To cope with human disturbances, they have learned to incorporate new items into their diet. In pine plantations, many primate species have been observed bark-stripping exotic trees to consume inner tissues, like phloem and cambium. While this adaptive behavior helps them survive in altered environments, it creates tension with forestry producers due to the economic damage caused to the trees.

Bark-stripping has long been considered a fallback strategy used when preferred food sources are scarce. However, evidence challenges this idea. In our study with Black capuchins in northeastern Argentina, we are trying to test if they target pine phloem when it becomes more "profitable", that is, when it’s easier to access and rich in sugary compounds. This suggests that bark-stripping might not simply be a last resort for survival, but rather a strategic dietary choice driven by seasonal changes in both quantity and quality of overall foods.

Conflict & Solutions

To reduce bark-stripping while protecting black capuchin monkeys, we conducted diversionary feeding experiments. We installed feeding platforms in native forest fragments and baited them with fruits during the bark-stripping season. The goal was to modify capuchin movement patterns and keep them away from sensitive pine stands. Since capuchins tend to avoid items that are far away, likely due to the high energy costs of arboreal locomotion, they began using areas near the platforms more frequently.

Positive results from diversionary feeding are likely linked to the capuchins' ability to remember important food sources, the costs of arboreal locomotion, and the energy trade-offs involved in their movement decisions. Our results suggest that diversionary feeding techniques can be an effective approach to promoting primate-friendly forestry. They also highlight the importance of understanding the ecology of populations living in production landscapes to improve management strategies aimed at biodiversity conservation.
